Scene 02

Text Storing and searching records

For a computer to work with text, reading was not enough. Words had to become editable, storable, searchable and comparable.

From readable text to stored information

Reading

A text begins as something to be read.

On the page, words form a continuous passage. Their meaning emerges through sentences, rhythm and context.

For a computer to work with the same material, the passage first had to be represented as editable characters inside a technical system.

Text editing

The passage becomes editable machine text.

Early environments such as RAX combined text editing, typewriter simulation and character-code functions.

Words were no longer fixed only on paper. Individual characters, lines and positions could be changed inside the system.

Source fragment

Information storage

A passage becomes a collection of records.

Once text could be entered into a system, it could also be divided, classified and retrieved.

FAMULUS treated information as stored records that could be organised through fields and keywords, rather than read only as one continuous document.

From retrieval to textual analysis

Electronic retrieval

Finding information becomes computational.

Electronic retrieval changed discovery itself. A query could be submitted to an indexed collection, and matching records could be returned.

The computer did not simply hold the material. It became part of the process through which information was located.

Concordance

A word becomes a pattern of contexts.

A concordance moved beyond locating a word. Each occurrence could be placed beside the language that surrounded it.

Repetition, context and difference became visible through alignment and comparison.

Scene 03

Language Beyond the standard character set

Text could be stored and searched, but not every language fitted easily into the characters that early systems expected.

Making more writing systems visible

A limited alphabet

Ordinary letters were not always enough.

Early systems were built around restricted character sets. For scholars working with historical languages, specialised symbols or non-Latin scripts, parts of a text could simply disappear.

The issue was no longer only whether text could be entered. It was whether the system could represent the writing in front of it.

Scene 04

Archive From catalogues to connected collections

Digital catalogues expanded the search for sources from one library collection to networked catalogues, repositories and online historical records.

From one catalogue to many collections

November 1988

The University catalogue moves online.

SAULCAT made the machine-readable St Andrews University Library Catalogue available through networked computing.

A catalogue describing one local collection could now be searched electronically rather than consulted only as a printed or locally maintained finding aid.

From catalogue records to digital resources

May 2005

Research begins to circulate through repositories.

Digital discovery increasingly led beyond a description of a source to research material that could be deposited, managed and accessed online.

The University Library’s ePrints initiative connected descriptive metadata with the circulation of research outputs.

Scene 05

Heritage Visualising places and objects

Digital methods made it possible to explore cultural material not only as records and texts, but also as visual patterns, mapped places, analysable images and reconstructed environments.

From patterns in text to places on a map

2013

Patterns across a text collection become visible.

Earlier systems allowed researchers to retrieve individual words and records. Topic visualisation offered another perspective by showing groups of related terms across much larger collections.

Instead of following one document from beginning to end, a researcher could move between themes and explore how different parts of a collection were connected.

Source fragment

TopicViz, probabilistic topic models, citation-graph exploration and large text collections.

2014–15

Literary texts are placed back into the city.

Palimpsest connected literary text data with locations across Edinburgh, treating references to streets, neighbourhoods and landmarks as spatial information.

LitLong developed this work into a public-facing literary map. A location could become an entry point into the texts and stories associated with that place.

From image to interpretation

2018

A scanned page becomes searchable text.

Early printed pages presented challenges that standard OCR systems were not designed to handle, including unusual layouts, non-standard characters and damaged material.

The Old French Bible Project used retraining workflows to identify textual regions and transform page images into digital text that could be searched and studied.

Source fragment

2019

A historical object becomes an analysable image.

Computer vision extended visual analysis beyond manuscript pages. Historical objects could also be represented as images and examined through their visible features.

Deep-learning methods applied to ancient Roman coins connected visual recognition with historical and numismatic research.

Source fragment

Deep learning and computer vision used to analyse ancient Roman coins.

From evidence to public heritage

2019

Evidence is assembled into a virtual place.

The Finlaggan project brought together historical research, drone footage, photogrammetry and digital modelling.

These different forms of evidence supported a reconstruction of the medieval home of the Lords of the Isles, connecting academic research with a public museum experience.

“created a digital reconstruction of the medieval home of the Lords of the Isles at Finlaggan on Islay”

— Virtual Reconstruction of Medieval Home of the Lords of the Isles, May 2019

2023–25

Digital heritage becomes a public experience.

Later projects examined how virtual museums and immersive exhibits could support interpretation, engagement and learning in heritage environments.

Digital heritage developed into a wider research field connecting technical methods with museums, communities, inclusive learning and public participation.
